## 🖼️ Slide 1 — Title Page *(0:00–0:45)*

> Good afternoon everyone. My name is Jingcheng Qian, and today I’ll be presenting my research titled *“Data Quality of Generative Images for Object Detection.”*
>
> This work sits at the intersection of **computer vision** and **generative AI**. It explores a very practical question:
>  Can images produced by *AI image generation models* actually help us train better visual recognition systems, especially when **real data is scarce or expensive to collect**?
>
> In this talk, I’ll start by explaining the background and challenges, then summarize related work, outline my research objectives, and finally walk you through the methodology and expected outcomes.

------

## 🌍 Slide 2 — The Data Bottleneck *(0:45–2:00)*

> Object detection — identifying and locating objects in an image — is a core task in many applications, from autonomous driving to robotics and surveillance.
>
> However, these models are **data-hungry**. They require thousands of manually annotated images, where each object must be labeled with bounding boxes and class names.
>
> This process is extremely **time-consuming and costly**, especially for industrial datasets.
>
> For instance, in an autonomous driving dataset like BDD100K, labeling one image with 10 objects can take up to 2 minutes per annotator. Multiply that by hundreds of thousands of images — and you can imagine the enormous human effort and expense.
>
> Traditional *data augmentation* methods, such as rotation, flipping, or color jittering, are helpful — but they only **transform existing pixels**. They don’t create *new* events, scenes, or rare scenarios.

> So the question becomes: what if we could **generate** those missing situations — safely, cheaply, and with perfect labels?

------

## ⚠️ Slide 3 — The Long-tail Challenge *(2:00–2:40)*

> In real-world data, some events occur frequently, like daytime driving on clear roads. But **rare and safety-critical events** — such as heavy rain, fog, or an ambulance approaching — are underrepresented.
>
> This imbalance is known as the **long-tail problem**: models trained mostly on common cases perform poorly in the rare but critical ones.
>
> For example, a detector may recognize cars easily during daytime, but fail at night or in foggy weather. This directly affects **safety and reliability**.
>
> So, instead of waiting for these rare scenes to occur, we can ask: *can we generate them synthetically?*

------

## 🪄 Slide 4 — Generative Models: A New Opportunity *(2:40–3:30)*

> Recent advances in **generative AI** — especially *diffusion models* — open exciting new possibilities.
>
> These models learn the underlying patterns of real images and can **generate new, realistic images** from text prompts or simple sketches.
>
> Think of them as an “AI photographer” who has seen millions of real photos and can now imagine new scenes that never existed before — such as “a car turning on a foggy mountain road at night.”
>
> These synthetic images are often indistinguishable from real ones, and they come with *automatic labels* if generated in a controlled way.
>
> This is potentially a **game-changer** for data-limited fields like autonomous driving.

> But here’s the key challenge — *do these AI-generated images truly help improve detection performance, or do they just look good to humans?*

------

## 📚 Slide 5 — Literature Review (Part I) *(3:30–4:30)*

> Let’s look at what previous work has done in this area.
>
> **First**, traditional augmentation techniques — such as MixUp, CutMix, or Copy-Paste — can enhance generalization but are limited to pixel-level changes. They cannot produce *new object relationships* or unseen scene compositions.
>
> **Second**, simulation-based datasets, like those from **CARLA** or **GTA-V**, can generate perfectly labeled data, but they look *synthetic* and often fail to capture the rich textures or lighting of the real world.
>
> **Third**, domain adaptation and style transfer methods try to reduce the visual gap between synthetic and real data. However, the *domain gap* — differences in color tone, texture, and scene realism — still persists.

------

## 📚 Slide 6 — Literature Review (Part II) *(4:30–5:30)*

> In recent years, **diffusion models** have surpassed GANs and VAEs in image quality and controllability.
>
> A key development here is **controllable generation** — the ability to guide where and how objects appear in the generated image.
>
> For example:
>
> - **ControlNet** allows us to feed structural information, like an edge map or depth map, so the generated image respects the original layout.
> - **LoRA**, which stands for *Low-Rank Adaptation*, enables lightweight fine-tuning. Instead of retraining a huge model, we only adjust a few internal layers to adapt the model to a new visual domain — like nighttime, foggy weather, or a specific dataset.
>
> Together, these advances make synthetic data generation *practical and flexible*.
>
> However, what’s still missing is a **systematic study** of how these components — ControlNet, LoRA, and others — affect **data quality** and, more importantly, **downstream detection performance**.

------

## 🎯 Slide 7 — Research Objectives *(5:30–6:15)*

> Based on these gaps, my research has five main objectives:
>
> 1. **Quantify** the domain gap between real and synthetic data using multiple metrics.
> 2. **Evaluate** how much synthetic data can improve detection models trained with limited real data.
> 3. **Assess** the realism, diversity, and annotation consistency of generated images.
> 4. **Analyze** the individual contribution of components such as ControlNet, LoRA, and filtering.
> 5. **Formulate** best practices for integrating generative data into practical training pipelines.
>
> In short — I want to understand *how useful synthetic data really is*, *when it helps*, and *why it helps*.

------

## ⚙️ Slide 8 — Research Framework *(6:15–7:15)*

> The research follows a **closed-loop framework**, combining generation, filtering, training, and evaluation.
>
> - In the **generation stage**, I use diffusion models with ControlNet and LoRA for controllable and adaptive synthesis.
> - The **quality filtering** stage selects high-quality samples using perceptual and semantic metrics like CLIP or DINOv2.
> - The **training** stage involves mixing real and synthetic images in different proportions.
> - Finally, the **evaluation** stage measures both image quality and detection accuracy, feeding results back to refine the generation process.
>
> This loop ensures that improvements are not just visual — they are *quantitatively validated*.

------

## 🧬 Slide 9 — Generation Pipeline *(7:15–8:30)*

> Here’s a closer look at the generation pipeline.
>
> I use **ComfyUI**, a modular, node-based workflow system that lets me combine and visualize each component — model, control, and output — in a flexible and reproducible way.
>
> The core model is **Stable Diffusion 3.5**, enhanced by **ControlNet** for spatial precision and **LoRA** for efficient domain fine-tuning.
>
> This setup lets me generate, for example, a sequence of highway scenes where the same car appears under different lighting or weather conditions — something impossible with traditional augmentation.
>
> By adjusting LoRA weights and ControlNet inputs, I can produce large-scale, diverse, and labeled data with minimal manual intervention.

------

## 🔍 Slide 10 — Training and Evaluation *(8:30–9:50)*

> Once the synthetic data is generated, I train three representative detection models: **YOLO**, **Faster R-CNN**, and **RT-DETR**.
>
> Each model is trained with different *real-to-synthetic* ratios — from 100% real data to as low as 10% real and 90% synthetic — to find the most cost-effective balance.
>
> The evaluation includes:
>
> - **Detection metrics:** mean Average Precision (mAP) and AP50;
> - **Generative quality metrics:** FID and IS for fidelity and diversity;
> - **Semantic metrics:** CLIP and DINOv2 similarity scores for label alignment;
> - **Human evaluation:** subjective realism and annotation accuracy.
>
> This multi-level evaluation connects *how real an image looks* to *how useful it actually is*.
